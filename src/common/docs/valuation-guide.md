# A Simple Guide to Valuing Stocks in the Indian Market

*Plain-language guide for individual investors. Not investment advice.*

---

## 1. The Core Idea

**Price is what you pay, value is what you get.** Valuation is just an estimate of what a business is worth, so you can judge whether the share price is cheap, fair, or expensive.

Three questions do most of the work:

1. **Is it a good business?** (quality: returns, debt, cash flow, management)
2. **Will it grow?** (growth: sales and profits over the next 3–5 years)
3. **Is the price reasonable?** (valuation: what you're paying for that quality and growth)

A great company at a crazy price can be a bad investment. An average company at a very low price can be a good one. You need all three.

---

## 2. The Three Families of Valuation Methods

| Method | What it means | Example metrics | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Relative valuation** | Compare to peers or to the stock's own history | P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales | Quick checks, most listed companies |
| **Absolute (intrinsic) valuation** | Estimate future cash flows and discount them to today | DCF, Dividend Discount Model | Stable, predictable businesses |
| **Asset-based valuation** | Value what the company owns | Book value, NAV, Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) | Banks, real estate, holding companies, conglomerates |

**Simple rule:** Start with relative valuation (fast), cross-check with one other method. If two methods roughly agree, you can be more confident.

---

## 3. When to Use Which Method

| Situation | Use this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Steady, profitable company (FMCG, IT, pharma) | **P/E + DCF** | Earnings are predictable |
| Bank, NBFC, or lender | **P/B + ROE** | Their "product" is money; book value matters most |
| Lots of debt or heavy assets (power, telecom, metals) | **EV/EBITDA** | Includes debt; ignores depreciation differences |
| Cyclical business (steel, cement, chemicals, auto) | **EV/EBITDA, P/B at different points of the cycle** | Profits swing wildly; P/E misleads |
| Loss-making or early-stage (new-age tech) | **EV/Sales + unit economics** | There's no profit to put a P/E on |
| High dividend payer (PSUs, utilities, ITC-type) | **Dividend yield / DDM** | Most of the return comes as dividends |
| Group with many businesses (Reliance, Tata-type holdcos, Bajaj Holdings) | **Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP)** | Value each piece separately, then apply a holdco discount (often 20–50%) |
| Real estate developer | **NAV + pre-sales** | Value lies in land bank and projects |
| Insurance company | **P/EV (life), P/E + combined ratio (general)** | Accounting profits don't capture future policy value |

## 4. Sector Cheat Sheet (Indian Market)

| Sector | Main valuation metric | Key things to track |
|---|---|---|
| **Private Banks** | P/B, P/ABV | ROE (>15%), ROA (>1%), GNPA/NNPA, CASA ratio (>40% is strong), NIM, credit cost, capital adequacy |
| **PSU Banks** | P/B (usually at a discount) | Same as above + govt. ownership, recapitalisation risk |
| **NBFCs / HFCs** | P/B | ROA (>2%), AUM growth, borrowing cost, asset quality, ALM mismatch |
| **Life Insurance** | P/EV | VNB margin, APE growth, persistency ratio |
| **General Insurance** | P/E, P/B | Combined ratio (<100% means underwriting profit), solvency ratio |
| **AMCs / Brokers / Exchanges** | P/E, Market cap as % of AUM | AUM growth, SIP flows, market share, active client count |
| **IT Services** | P/E, FCF yield | Constant-currency revenue growth, EBIT margin, deal wins (TCV), attrition, US/Europe demand |
| **FMCG / Consumer** | P/E (premium sector) | Volume growth (not just price-led), rural demand, gross margins, distribution reach |
| **Pharma** | P/E, EV/EBITDA | US FDA inspection results, product pipeline, domestic vs export mix, R&D spend |
| **Hospitals / Diagnostics** | EV/EBITDA | ARPOB, occupancy, bed additions |
| **Auto & Auto Ancillary** | P/E, EV/EBITDA | Monthly volumes, EV transition, raw material costs, inventory at dealers |
| **Capital Goods / Infra / Defence** | P/E | Order book-to-sales ratio, execution, working capital days, govt. capex |
| **Cement** | EV/EBITDA, EV/tonne | EBITDA per tonne, capacity utilisation, fuel costs, regional pricing |
| **Metals & Mining** | EV/EBITDA, P/B | Global commodity prices, China demand, debt levels |
| **Oil & Gas** | EV/EBITDA, P/E, Dividend yield | GRMs (refiners), crude prices, govt. pricing controls |
| **Power / Utilities** | P/B, Dividend yield | Regulated ROE, PLF, renewable pipeline, receivables from state DISCOMs |
| **Telecom** | EV/EBITDA | ARPU, subscriber adds, debt, spectrum costs |
| **Real Estate** | NAV, P/B | Pre-sales (bookings), collections, land bank, debt |
| **Retail** | EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA | Same-store sales growth, revenue per sq. ft., store additions |
| **Hotels** | EV/EBITDA | RevPAR, occupancy, average room rate |
| **Specialty Chemicals** | P/E, EV/EBITDA | ROCE, China+1 demand, capacity expansion, client concentration |
| **New-age Tech / Platforms** | EV/Sales, EV/GMV | Contribution margin, cash burn, path to profitability, customer retention |

**Golden rule for cyclicals (metals, cement, sugar, chemicals):** P/E often looks *lowest* at the peak of the cycle (profits are huge) and *highest* at the bottom (profits are tiny). So a low P/E in a cyclical can be a trap, and a high P/E can be an opportunity.

## 5. Key Metrics Explained Simply

### Valuation metrics (Is it cheap?)

| Metric | Formula | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **P/E** | Price ÷ EPS | How many years of current profit you're paying for. Compare with peers and the stock's own 5–10 year average. |
| **PEG** | P/E ÷ Earnings growth % | Around 1 or below = reasonably priced for its growth. Above 2 = expensive. |
| **P/B** | Price ÷ Book value per share | Most useful for banks and asset-heavy firms. Justified by ROE: higher ROE deserves higher P/B. |
| **EV/EBITDA** | (Market cap + Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA | Fairer than P/E when companies have different debt levels. |
| **EV/Sales** | EV ÷ Revenue | For loss-making or low-margin firms. |
| **Dividend Yield** | Dividend per share ÷ Price | 3–5%+ is high in India. Check if the payout is sustainable. |
| **Earnings Yield** | EPS ÷ Price (i.e., 1 ÷ P/E) | Compare with the 10-year Government bond (G-Sec) yield. If the stock's earnings yield is far below the bond yield, you're relying heavily on growth. |
| **FCF Yield** | Free cash flow ÷ Market cap | Real cash the business generates per ₹100 invested. |

### Quality metrics (Is it a good business?)

| Metric | Rough benchmark | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **ROE** | > 15% | How well the company uses shareholders' money |
| **ROCE** | > 15% (and higher than cost of borrowing) | How well it uses *all* capital, including debt |
| **Debt-to-Equity** | < 1 for non-financial companies | Too much debt = risk in bad times |
| **Interest Coverage** | > 3x | Can it comfortably pay interest? |
| **Cash from Operations ÷ Net Profit** | Close to or above 1 over 3–5 years | Are profits turning into real cash? |
| **Working Capital Days** | Stable or falling | Rising receivables or inventory can hide trouble |
| **Sales & Profit Growth** | Consistent over 5 years | One good year isn't a trend |

## 6. A Quick Note on DCF (Kept Simple)

DCF says: *a company is worth all the cash it will generate in future, adjusted for the fact that future money is worth less than money today.*

Typical Indian assumptions (adjust to your view):

- **Discount rate (cost of equity):** roughly 11–14%. This is the 10-year G-Sec yield plus an extra 5–7% for equity risk. Use the higher end for smaller or riskier companies.
- **Terminal growth rate:** keep it conservative, around 4–6%. Never above the discount rate.
- **Forecast period:** 5–10 years.

**Practical tip: try a "reverse DCF."** Instead of predicting the future, ask: *"What growth rate is the current price already assuming?"* If the market price implies 25% growth for 15 years, ask yourself whether that's realistic. Screener.in and similar tools make this easy.

DCF is very sensitive to assumptions. Small changes in growth or discount rate can change the value by 30–50%. Use it as a sense-check, not as an exact answer.

## 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **"Low P/E = cheap."** Not always. It may be low because the business is declining, the profits are one-off, or the sector is at a cyclical peak.
2. **Comparing across sectors.** A P/E of 50 is normal for some FMCG companies and absurd for a steel company. Always compare within the same sector.
3. **Ignoring debt.** Two companies with the same P/E can have very different risk if one carries heavy debt. Use EV/EBITDA to see the full picture.
4. **Trusting one year's numbers.** Look at 5–10 year trends.
5. **Falling for a value trap.** A stock that's cheap and stays cheap, often due to governance issues or a shrinking business.
6. **Overpaying for "quality."** Great businesses bought at extreme valuations can deliver flat returns for years.
7. **Ignoring the "why now?"** Know what could make the market re-rate the stock: earnings growth, debt reduction, new product, sector turnaround.

## 8. A Simple Step-by-Step Process

1. **Understand the business.** What does it sell, who buys it, and why would customers choose it over competitors? If you can't explain it in two lines, skip it.
2. **Identify the sector.** Pick the right valuation metric from the cheat sheet in Section 4.
3. **Check quality.** ROE/ROCE, debt, cash flow vs profit, 5-year growth.
4. **Check management.** Promoter holding, pledging, governance red flags.
5. **Value it with two methods.** For example, P/E versus peers and history, plus a reverse DCF.
6. **Set a fair value range,** not a single number (e.g., ₹900–1,100).
7. **Demand a margin of safety.** Aim to buy 20–30% below your fair value estimate, more for riskier companies.
8. **Write down your reason for buying** and what would make you sell. Review every quarter after results.

### Mini example (relative valuation)

- Company A's EPS this year: ₹50
- Expected EPS next year: ₹58 (16% growth)
- Peers trade at 22x forward earnings; Company A's own 5-year average is 20x
- Use a conservative 20x → Fair value ≈ 20 × ₹58 = **₹1,160**
- Current price: ₹900 → about 22% below fair value → worth a closer look (if quality checks pass)

---

## Quick Summary

- Pick the **method** based on the business type: P/E for steady earners, P/B for lenders, EV/EBITDA for debt-heavy or cyclical firms, EV/Sales for loss-makers, SOTP for groups.
- Always compare **within the same sector** and against the stock's **own history**.
- **Quality and management** matter as much as the valuation number, especially in India.
- Use a **range, not a single number**, and buy with a **margin of safety**.
