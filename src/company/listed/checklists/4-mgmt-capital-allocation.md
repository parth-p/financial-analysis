# Management & Capital Allocation — Analyst's Checklist

*Companion to the Business Model (v2), Industry Structure, and Moat checklists. Step 4 of the framework.*

> **The one rule for this step:** this is **judgment about people and decisions** — two questions wearing one label:
> 1. Are they good **operators** (run the business well)?
> 2. Are they good **capital allocators** (deploy the cash it throws off well)?
>
> The second is the neglected one and often matters *more* over a decade — executives rise through operations and are then handed a capital-allocation job they've never practiced, so it's frequently the weakest muscle in the most powerful hands.
>
> **The governing discipline: behavior over words.** Where the cash actually went, how they're actually paid, what they bought with their own money — these beat any letter, interview, or deck. Watch what they do, not what they say.
>
> **Boundary:** management quality is *distinct* from business quality (Steps 1–3) — don't let a great business halo weak managers, or a great manager halo a bad business.

### The flag convention (same as before)
- **Format:** `Fact → Step N`
- ✅ "Reliance on adjusted EBITDA that strips recurring costs — quantify the gap → Step 5"
- ✅ "Promoter pledging at 40% of holding → Step 7 (size the risk)"

**Who owns which judgment:** deep quality-of-earnings / three-statement math → **Step 5** · whether the moat is priced in → **Step 6** · severity & probability of a governance/integrity risk → **Step 7** · the thesis → **Step 8**. Step 4 keeps the *character and decision* read.

**Incoming flags to resolve here (from Step 3):** any advantage that looked like execution rather than structure — is it repeatable, and how person-dependent? And: does management actually reinvest well at high ROIC (testing the moat's reinvestment runway against reality)?

**How to use this:** grade decisions and incentives from *evidence of behavior*, not narrative. Output is a two-part verdict (operators / allocators) plus parked flags.

---

## 1. Capital allocation track record — *where did the cash go, and did each use earn its keep?* (the heart)
Look at where cash *actually* went over a full cycle (5–10 years) and grade each use.
- [ ] **Reinvestment:** did organic capex/R&D earn returns above cost of capital? *(Tests Step 3's reinvestment runway against reality.)*
- [ ] **M&A:** prices paid, integration, did deals earn their cost of capital? *(Base rate: most M&A destroys value — serial acquirers get scrutiny, not credit.)*
- [ ] **Buybacks:** bought back *cheap* (accretive) or at peaks / merely offsetting stock-comp dilution? Is there real net share reduction?
- [ ] **Dividends:** appropriate given the reinvestment opportunity set? (Fat dividend + high-return runway = mistake; dividend + no good options = right.)
- [ ] **Debt:** sensible given the balance sheet and cost of debt?
- [ ] **The meta-tell — counter-cyclical or pro-cyclical?** Great allocators invest/buy back in downturns and hold/pay down in booms; poor ones do the reverse.

**The anchoring question:** over the last 5–10 years, where did *every* dollar of cash go, and would a thoughtful owner have done the same?

---

## 2. Incentives — *how are they paid?*
Incentives predict behavior better than any stated value.
- [ ] Compensated on what? **EPS** (gameable via buybacks/leverage) / **revenue or size** (empire-building) / **share price** (short-termism) — vs. **ROIC / per-share value / multi-year TSR** (aligned)?
- [ ] Targets genuinely stretching, or sandbagged to always pay out?
- [ ] Vesting/time horizon — short-term or long-term?

**The test:** if you knew *only* the comp plan, what behavior would you predict — and does it serve shareholders?

---

## 3. Ownership & skin in the game
- [ ] Do management and the board own stock *meaningfully* — relative to their net worth, not a token grant?
- [ ] **Bought vs. granted:** open-market purchases with their own money (strong positive signal) vs. handed grants (not).
- [ ] Founder-led? Often a long-term-orientation positive — but note key-person risk and family/promoter governance questions.
- [ ] Insider buying/selling pattern (buys are strong signal; selling is noisier).

---

## 4. Operating competence & the say-do ratio
- [ ] Execution evidence: margins and delivery vs. peers; hitting operational targets.
- [ ] **The say-do ratio (best behavioral test):** pull guidance/promises from 3–5 years ago and compare to what actually happened. Chronic over-promising is deeply informative and rarely priced in.
- [ ] How do they handle adversity and mistakes — candor or spin?

---

## 5. Candor & accounting character
- [ ] Do they communicate honestly, *including bad news*? Do they discuss mistakes openly (rare, valuable)?
- [ ] Shareholder letters/calls: substance vs. promotional fluff.
- [ ] Accounting posture: do they lean on adjusted/non-GAAP metrics that strip *real, recurring* costs? Aggressive revenue recognition?

**Boundary:** the deep quality-of-earnings math is **Step 5**. Here you read the numbers for *character* — are they trying to inform you or impress you? Flag the specifics `→ Step 5`.

---

## 6. Governance & alignment structures *(load-bearing for founder/promoter/SME names)*
- [ ] Board independence and quality; dual-class or control structures.
- [ ] **Promoter pledging** of shares (serious red flag). **Related-party transactions** / tunneling risk.
- [ ] Auditor quality; any auditor *resignation* or change (a klaxon).
- [ ] History of shareholder-hostile actions (dilution, sweetheart deals).
- [ ] Any integrity/legal overhang (litigation, regulatory action, FIRs on billing/revenue) — surface it *prominently*; integrity issues can invalidate every number downstream.

**Boundary:** note it here; the *severity and probability* → **Step 7**.

---

## 7. Skill vs. tailwind — *the discipline check* (rule out the masqueraders)
Before crediting management with anything, rule out that the results came from something else:
- [ ] A great **industry** (Step 2) or **moat** (Step 3) doing the work while they held the wheel.
- [ ] A **cyclical tailwind.**
- [ ] The **predecessor's** decisions still paying off.

**The isolating question:** which outcomes trace to *discretionary decisions they made* (capital allocation is cleanest — it's genuinely their choice) vs. what any competent caretaker would have produced?

---

## Traps specific to this step (process errors — re-read before finishing)
- [ ] **The halo effect** — charisma / media-darling / articulate ≠ good capital allocation. Storytelling skill and allocation skill are unrelated.
- [ ] Judging managers by results in a business whose *tailwind* did the work.
- [ ] Recency bias from one good or bad year.
- [ ] Over-weighting the polished narrative (letters can be PR) vs. the behavior (where cash actually went, insider buys with own money).

---

## The management verdict (do this last)
State it in two lines — the analog of the earlier verdict tests:

> **Operators:** [strong / adequate / weak], because ________________________.
> **Capital allocators:** [strong / adequate / weak], because ________________________.
> Incentives & ownership: [aligned / mixed / misaligned].

If you can't back each with a specific *decision* or *behavior* (not a quality or a vibe), you're rating the story, not the people. Go back to Sections 1 and 4.

---

## Handoff to the rest of the framework
Step 4 produces a two-part verdict + parked flags. Everything tagged `→ Step N` collects here, grouped by owner:

- **→ Step 5 (financials):** accounting-character flags to verify (adjusted-metric gaps, revenue recognition); reinvestment ROIC to quantify; buyback timing vs. price to check.
- **→ Step 6 (valuation):** if capital allocation is poor, discount the value of retained earnings — a dollar reinvested badly isn't worth a dollar.
- **→ Step 7 (risks):** governance/integrity risks (pledging, related-party, litigation/FIRs, key-person) — sized and probability-weighted there.
- **→ Step 8 (thesis):** is management a *pillar* of the thesis (a proven allocator compounding capital) or a *risk* to underwrite around? State which.

*Steps 1–3 judged the business. Step 4 judges the people steering its cash — and separates their skill from the tailwind they're riding.*
