# Moat — Analyst's Checklist

*Companion to the Business Model (v2) and Industry Structure checklists. Step 3 of the framework.*

> **The one rule for this step:** a moat is the **durability of *excess* returns** — the reason this company keeps earning *above its cost of capital and above the average competitor* instead of having it competed away. It is **your license to extrapolate**: without a moat, the default assumption is that today's good returns decay toward the cost of capital, and Step 6 must value it that way.
>
> **The two-sided boundary:**
> - **Moat ≠ industry attractiveness (Step 2).** Step 2 was true of the *average* player. A moat is what makes *this* company beat that average, durably. If your claim is true of everyone in the industry, it's the pond — send it back to Step 2.
> - **Moat ≠ current profitability (Step 5) and ≠ good management (Step 4).** High returns now are a snapshot; a moat is *persistence*. Great execution is fragile unless it's structural.

### The flag convention (same as before)
- **Format:** `Fact → Step N`
- ✅ "Detailed ROIC reconciliation needed to confirm the return pattern → Step 5"
- ❌ deciding the stock is cheap because the moat is wide (that's Step 6)

**Who owns which judgment:** detailed return/quality-of-earnings math → **Step 5** · management/execution quality → **Step 4** · whether the moat is priced in → **Step 6** · whether erosion breaks the thesis & how likely → **Step 7** · the differentiated thesis → **Step 8**.

**The central hand-off you're resolving here:** Step 2 named the industry's *key success factors* ("winning here requires X"). Step 3 asks: **does this company actually have X — and durably?** If your Step 2 flags don't get answered here, the two steps aren't wired together.

**How to use this:** numbers before narrative. Look for the moat in the financials *first* (Section 1); only then name it, size it, and judge its direction. The output is a one-line moat verdict plus parked flags.

---

## 1. Evidence — *is there a moat at all?* (numbers before narrative)
Look for the fingerprint before you tell yourself the story.
- [ ] **ROIC persistently above cost of capital**, across many years — and *stable*, ideally holding through a downturn. (One year is luck; a decade is a moat casting a shadow.)
- [ ] **Pricing power:** can they raise prices without losing volume? Clean tell — did they pass through inflation while holding margin?
- [ ] **Market share:** durable or rising over time, especially in downturns?
- [ ] **Gross margin:** stable and above peers?

**The discipline:** if the moat isn't visible in the numbers, distrust the story. "Strong brand" with no pricing power isn't a moat; "sticky product" with high churn isn't a moat.

**Boundary:** you're using the *pattern* (high + stable) as evidence a moat exists. The detailed ROIC reconciliation → **Step 5**.

---

## 2. Source — *name which of the five*
If a moat exists, name its mechanism honestly. Can't name which one? You don't have a moat — you have a good year.
- [ ] **Switching costs** — cost/pain/risk/downtime to leave (integration depth, data lock-in, retraining, mission-criticality). *(This is where Step 1's criticality flag resolves: criticality → switching costs → pricing power.)*
- [ ] **Network effects** — the product gets more valuable as more users join. Strongest moat when real; self-reinforcing toward winner-take-most.
- [ ] **Intangibles** — brand, patents, licenses, regulatory approvals. *Brand counts only if it changes willingness-to-pay or churn — awareness alone does not.*
- [ ] **Cost advantage** — structurally lower cost (scale, process, location, unique asset access). Must be *structural*, not "efficient right now" (that's execution → Step 4).
- [ ] **Efficient scale** — market just big enough that a new entrant makes everyone unprofitable, so rational players don't enter.
- [ ] **Stacked?** Do two or more sources reinforce each other? (Deeper and rarer.)

---

## 3. Width & depth — *how much it protects, how big the advantage*
- [ ] **Width:** how much of the business/customer base does the moat actually shield — all of it, or one segment?
- [ ] **Depth:** how big is the edge? Quantify — margin points of cost advantage, months of switching pain, % price premium, share-of-category from the network.

**A moat you can't size is a moat you're guessing at.**

---

## 4. Direction — *widening, stable, or eroding* (the whole ballgame)
- [ ] Over the next ~5 years, is the moat getting **stronger** (scale/data flywheel, deepening integration) or **weaker** (tech shift, patent cliff, commoditization, new entrant with a different model)?
- [ ] *(Pull in Step 2's structural-trajectory flag here.)*

**A shrinking moat with great current numbers is a value trap in slow motion — the most expensive error in this step.**

---

## 5. Durability & replicability — *how long, and against whom*
- [ ] What's the half-life of *this* source? (Patents expire on schedule; brands can last a century; network effects can flip overnight when a better network appears.)
- [ ] **The master test:** what would it take for a well-capitalized competitor to replicate this — how much money, how many years — and *why haven't they already?*

If a rival with a known budget and a couple of years could rebuild it, the moat is shallow regardless of the current numbers.

---

## 6. Reinvestment runway — *can the moat absorb more capital?* (moat × compounding)
- [ ] Is this a **reinvestment moat** (can keep deploying earnings at high ROIC — a long runway) or a **static fortress** (protects a cash cow but can't absorb more capital)?
- [ ] Does growth happen *inside* the moat (compounds value) or *outside* it (funds competitors' price wars)?

*The best moats aren't just walls — they're walls you can keep building higher at high returns.*

---

## 7. Moat vs. imposter — *rule out the four masqueraders*
Most cited "moats" are one of these in disguise. Actively rule each out:
- [ ] **Execution / management** → **Step 4.** "They just run it better" is fragile unless structural.
- [ ] **Size** → big isn't a moat unless size *confers* scale economies or network effects.
- [ ] **Growth** → a fast grower with no moat destroys value as it grows.
- [ ] **Cyclical peak** → top-of-cycle returns look exactly like a moat until the cycle turns.

---

## Traps specific to this step (process errors — re-read before finishing)
- [ ] Storytelling a moat that isn't in the numbers.
- [ ] The halo effect: successful company → *assuming* a moat instead of finding the mechanism.
- [ ] Backward-looking: the moat *was* real but is eroding now.
- [ ] Overrating brand (awareness ≠ pricing power).

---

## The moat verdict (do this last)
State it in one line — the analog of Step 1's two-sentence test and Step 2's industry verdict:

> Moat: **[none / narrow / wide]**, sourced from ________________, currently **[widening / stable / eroding]**, durable for roughly ________, because ________________________________.

If you can't name the **source** and answer the **replication question**, you have a story, not a moat. Go back to Sections 1–2.

---

## Handoff to the rest of the framework
Step 3 produces a moat verdict + parked flags. Everything tagged `→ Step N` collects here, grouped by owner:

- **→ Step 4 (management):** any advantage that's really execution rather than structure — is it repeatable, and does it depend on specific people?
- **→ Step 5 (financials):** confirm the return pattern (ROIC vs. WACC over time); quantify the depth (margin/pricing evidence); test durability against the numbers.
- **→ Step 6 (valuation):** the moat verdict *is* your license to extrapolate — feed the direction (widening/stable/eroding) straight into the terminal assumptions.
- **→ Step 7 (risks):** the specific erosion vectors from Sections 4–5 — sized and probability-weighted there.
- **→ Step 8 (thesis):** if there's no moat, the thesis can't rest on durable compounding — it must rest on price/cyclical/catalyst instead. Note that now.

*Step 2 judged the water. Step 3 judged whether this fish can keep out-swimming the school — and for how long.*
