# Business Model — Analyst's Checklist

*Step 1 of the framework.*

> **The one rule for this step:** describe the machine, don't judge it. You're not deciding if the business is *good* or *cheap* yet — you're building a picture so accurate that the moat, the financials, and the risks almost announce themselves. If you catch yourself writing "this is a great business," you've jumped ahead. Pull back to description.

### The flag convention (same in every step)
When you notice something that matters but isn't Step 1's to judge, write it as a **fact plus the step that owns it**, then move on.
- **Format:** `Fact → Step N`
- ✅ "Product is mission-critical: a failed part halts the customer's line → Step 3 (does that become switching costs?)"
- ❌ "Customers are locked in because the product is mission-critical" (that's a moat verdict — Step 3)

**Who owns which judgment (same in every step):**

| Step | Owns |
|---|---|
| 1 Business model | Description of the machine — no verdicts |
| 2 Industry structure | Verdict on the industry (the average player) |
| 3 Return durability | Moat (structural case) + credibility of guidance (asserted case) |
| 4 Management & capital allocation | Operators, allocators, incentives, character, governance |
| 5 Financials | Return/earnings math, ROIC vs. cost of capital, quality of earnings, bottom-up forecast |
| 6 Valuation | What's priced in; terminal assumptions |
| 7 Risks | Severity & probability of specific risks; kill-triggers |
| 8 Thesis & catalysts | The differentiated thesis; dated catalysts |

Step 1 owns no verdicts — only the description.

**How to use this:** Run every section. Where you *can't* answer a question, that gap is itself a finding — write it down as an open question for later steps. The goal isn't ticking boxes; it's being able to explain the business to a smart 15-year-old in two sentences by the end.

---

## 1. Unit of value — *what the customer actually pays for*
- [ ] What is the atomic transaction? (One customer, one purchase — what changed hands and why?)
- [ ] What the product/service actually is, and its concrete use cases. This full description lives here — be specific and complete. (Describe it; don't yet judge how good or defensible it is.)
- [ ] What "job" does the customer hire this product to do? (They buy the hole, not the drill.)
- [ ] How critical is it to the customer? Record the fact on a spectrum: mission-critical (the line halts / the batch is ruined if it fails) vs. nice-to-have. Note it and stop — whether that criticality turns into switching costs or pricing power is the moat question, not in scope here → Step 3.
- [ ] Which end-user industry buys it? Name it as a fact (who the customer is). Whether that industry is attractive, growing, or cyclical → Step 2.
- [ ] What the company *sells* vs. what the customer *buys* — are these the same thing?
- [ ] Who is the real competition for that job? (Often a different product than you'd assume.)

**Flag:** you can't explain the business simply. Complexity that resists a two-sentence explanation is information, not detail — record it as an open question.

---

## 2. Revenue model & quality — *how money comes in, and what kind*
- [ ] Recurring (contractual, must cancel), repeating (habitual re-purchase), or one-time (re-won each sale)?
- [ ] Usage-based or fixed? Does the customer relationship deepen over time or reset to zero?
- [ ] **How is price set?** List price, cost-plus, fixed-price contract, negotiated, or tender (L1)? Any raw-material pass-through or escalation clauses, and how often does price reset? (Describe the mechanism; whether it confers pricing power → Step 3.)
- [ ] **Key question:** On Jan 1, how much of this year's revenue does the company already effectively "have" vs. must go win again?

**Quality hierarchy:** recurring > repeating > one-time. Know where this company sits.

---

## 3. Customer & concentration — *who pays, and how dependent*
- [ ] Segment the customers. Who is the *marginal* (next) customer, and is that pool getting easier or harder to reach?
- [ ] Concentration on every axis: customer (one client >X% of revenue?), product, geography, channel.
- [ ] **Is the payer the same as the user?** (Ads, enterprise IT, anything "free" — the customer often isn't who you'd think.)

**Flag:** any single dependency >20–30% of revenue → Step 7. Hidden concentration = hidden fragility.

---

## 4. Cost structure & unit economics — *what one unit costs to deliver*
- [ ] Split costs: fixed vs. variable. (Determines operating leverage *and* downturn cyclicality.)
- [ ] Use the model's *native* unit metric — not generic margins:
  - Subscription → LTV:CAC ratio + payback period
  - Retail → per-store / per-square-foot economics
  - Marketplace → take rate
- [ ] What does it cost to acquire one customer, and how long to earn that back?

**Flag:** loses money per unit and plans to "make it up on volume" → Step 5.

---

## 5. Scalability — *the marginal-unit question*
- [ ] What happens to the cost of the *next* unit as the company grows? (Near-zero like software, or linear like a restaurant chain?)
- [ ] To double revenue, what does the company have to double — servers (cheap) or locations + headcount (expensive)?

**The distinction:** a business that *compounds* vs. one that just *gets bigger*.

---

## 6. Capital intensity & cash cycle — *the most-skipped section*
- [ ] Asset-light or asset-heavy? How much capital sunk per dollar of revenue?
- [ ] **Cash conversion cycle:** does the company get paid *before* or *after* it pays its own suppliers/costs?
- [ ] **The big one:** does growth *consume* cash (must fund inventory/receivables before customers pay) or *throw off* cash (customers fund the growth)?

**Don't miss:** negative working capital / float — collecting cash upfront (subscriptions, insurance premiums, Costco-style inventory turns). Easy to overlook and economically powerful when present; record it as a fact → Step 5 (confirm in the numbers).

---

## 7. Value chain position & power — *who can squeeze whom*
- [ ] Where does the company sit from raw input to end customer? What does it do itself vs. outsource?
- [ ] Dependencies: single supplier? Single platform landlord (App Store, Google Search, Amazon marketplace)? Single distributor?
- [ ] Who sets the terms the company operates on — its input prices, its selling prices, its access to customers? List them as facts. (Whether they can squeeze margins → Step 2; whether *this* company resists → Step 3.)

**Flag:** lives on a platform it doesn't control — a landlord who can raise the rent or evict → Step 2 & Step 7.

---

## 8. Growth mechanics — *how it actually gets bigger*
- [ ] Which lever(s)? More customers / more revenue per customer (price or upsell) / new products / new geographies.
- [ ] Each lever has different economics and durability — name the specific one this business pulls.
- [ ] **Strip out acquisitions and price hikes — is the underlying (same customers, same products) business actually growing?**

**Flag:** headline growth that evaporates once you remove M&A and pricing, or growth that requires constantly replacing churned customers → Step 5.

---

## Model-level flags (before touching a single financial)
- [ ] Revenue you can't trace to a clear customer paying for clear value → Step 5
- [ ] A model that only works at a scale it hasn't reached → Step 5 & Step 7
- [ ] Dependence on a channel/platform the company doesn't control → Step 2 & Step 7
- [ ] Management itself struggles to explain the business simply → Step 4

---

## Traps specific to this step (re-read before you finish)
- [ ] Judging instead of describing — "great business," "strong moat," "cheap." Route it, don't write it.
- [ ] Adopting the company's own framing of what business it's in (investor deck, RHP). Describe what the customer *buys*, not what management says it *sells*.
- [ ] Skipping capital intensity and the cash cycle (§6) — the most-skipped section.

---

## The two-sentence test (do this last)
Write the business in two sentences a smart 15-year-old would understand:

> _______________________________________________

> _______________________________________________

If you can't — you don't understand it yet. Stop and fix that before moving to Step 2.

---

## Handoff to the rest of the framework
Step 1 produces a description + a stack of parked flags. Everything tagged `→ Step N` collects here, grouped by owner:

- **→ Step 2 (industry):** the end-user industry; the real competition for the job; who sets the terms (suppliers, platforms, distributors).
- **→ Step 3 (return durability):** criticality to the customer; recurring revenue; how price is set (pass-through, escalation, tender); any switching or pricing-power hints.
- **→ Step 4 (management):** management struggling to explain the business simply.
- **→ Step 5 (financials):** unit economics, cash cycle, capital intensity, organic vs. headline growth — to confirm in the numbers.
- **→ Step 7 (risks):** concentration on any axis; platform/channel dependence; a model that only works at a scale it hasn't reached.

*Description here → judgment there.*
