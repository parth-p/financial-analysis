# Return Durability — Moat & Guidance

*Companion to Steps 1–2. Step 3 of the framework. (Replaces the earlier "Moat" checklist.)*

> **The one rule:** this step answers a single question — **will the excess returns persist, and how much should I believe it?** You weigh two bodies of evidence:
> - **The structural case — the *moat*** (Part A): why returns resist competition. Hard evidence, read from the numbers.
> - **The asserted case — *guidance & commentary*** (Part B): what management *claims* about the future. A claim to test, never an input to trust.
>
> **The real work is the reconciliation:** does the structural case underwrite the asserted one, or is management promising durability the moat can't deliver?
>
> **Boundary:** durability of returns is *not* industry attractiveness (Step 2 — the average player), *not* current profitability (Step 5 — a snapshot), *not* the full management verdict (Step 4). The moat is structural; guidance is a claim.

**Flag convention (same in every step):** `Fact → Step N`.

**Who owns which judgment (same in every step):**

| Step | Owns |
|---|---|
| 1 Business model | Description of the machine — no verdicts |
| 2 Industry structure | Verdict on the industry (the average player) |
| 3 Return durability | Moat (structural case) + credibility of guidance (asserted case) |
| 4 Management & capital allocation | Operators, allocators, incentives, character, governance |
| 5 Financials | Return/earnings math, ROIC vs. cost of capital, quality of earnings, bottom-up forecast |
| 6 Valuation | What's priced in; terminal assumptions |
| 7 Risks | Severity & probability of specific risks; kill-triggers |
| 8 Thesis & catalysts | The differentiated thesis; dated catalysts |

Step 3 keeps the durability verdict and the reconciliation.

**How to use:** Part A — *numbers before narrative.* Part B — *narrative as a claim,* tested against Part A, never the reverse. Output: a two-part verdict + the reconciliation. Where the evidence isn't available (no multi-year return history, no guidance track record, a moat you can't size), record it as an open finding rather than estimating to fill the gap. An unsized moat or an unbuildable say-do record is a result, not a blank.

# PART A — The structural case: the moat

## A1. Evidence — is there a moat at all? (numbers before narrative)
- [ ] **ROIC above cost of capital**, many years, *stable* (ideally through a downturn). One year is luck; a decade is a shadow.
- [ ] **Pricing power** — raise price without losing volume? (Clean tell: passed through inflation holding margin.)
- [ ] **Market share** — durable/rising, especially in downturns?
- [ ] **Gross margin** — stable and above peers?
- [ ] **Retention / churn** — do customers stay (renewal rates, tenure of key accounts, wallet share held)? The most direct test of switching costs.

**Discipline:** if it's not in the numbers, distrust the story. Detailed ROIC reconciliation → **Step 5**.

**Young companies (e.g., IPOs with 3–4 years of history):** "many years" doesn't exist yet. Look for leading indicators instead — improving unit economics, rising retention, price held or raised, share gains — and grade the moat **emerging**, not *none*. Record the missing history as a gap.

## A2. Source — name which of the six
Can't name one? You have a good year, not a moat.
- [ ] **Switching costs** (integration, data lock-in, retraining, mission-criticality). *Resolves Step 1's criticality flag.*
- [ ] **Network effects** — more users → more value; self-reinforcing.
- [ ] **Intangibles** — brand/patents/licenses. *Brand counts only if it moves willingness-to-pay or churn.*
- [ ] **Cost advantage** — structural (scale/process/location/asset access), not "efficient now" (→ Step 4).
- [ ] **Efficient scale** — market too small to profitably invite a new entrant.
- [ ] **Counter-positioning** — a business model incumbents can't copy without damaging their own core business.
- [ ] **Stacked?** Two+ sources reinforcing = deeper, rarer.

## A3. Width & depth
- [ ] **Width:** whole business or one segment?
- [ ] **Depth:** quantify — cost-advantage margin points, months of switching pain, % price premium. *A moat you can't size is one you're guessing at.*

## A4. Direction — widening / stable / eroding (the whole ballgame)
- [ ] Over ~5 yrs, strengthening (flywheel, deepening integration) or weakening (tech shift, patent cliff, commoditization, new model)? *Pull in Step 2's trajectory flag.*

*A shrinking moat with great current numbers is a value trap in slow motion.*

## A5. Durability & replicability
- [ ] Half-life of *this* source? (Patents expire; brands persist; networks can flip overnight.)
- [ ] **Master test:** what money + years would a well-funded rival need to rebuild it — and *why haven't they?* If a known budget and a couple of years could, it's shallow.

## A6. Reinvestment runway (moat × compounding)
- [ ] **Reinvestment moat** (redeploy earnings at high ROIC — long runway) or **static fortress** (protects a cash cow, can't absorb capital)?
- [ ] Growth *inside* the moat (compounds) or *outside* it (funds rivals' price wars)?

## A7. Moat vs. imposter — rule out the five masqueraders
- [ ] **Execution/management** → Step 4. "They just run it better" is fragile.
- [ ] **Size** → not a moat unless it confers scale economies or network effects.
- [ ] **Growth** → fast growth with no moat destroys value.
- [ ] **Cyclical peak** → top-of-cycle returns mimic a moat until the cycle turns.
- [ ] **Policy-created returns** → subsidies, protective duties, or incentive schemes that inflate returns look structural until the policy lapses. Not a moat unless the advantage survives without it. *(A license that limits entry is an intangible — A2; a subsidy that lifts margins is not. Pull in Step 2's regulation flag.)*

# PART B — The asserted case: guidance & commentary

> Management's forward guidance is a **claim, not evidence.** Part B's job is to test that claim — for credibility, for honesty of language, and for whether it reconciles with the structural reality of Part A. **Never anchor your own view to it.**

## B1. Inventory — what are they actually claiming?
Gather and categorize before judging.
- [ ] **Quantitative guidance:** revenue, margin, EPS, volume, capex, order-book/backlog, medium-term targets.
- [ ] **Qualitative commentary:** demand outlook, strategic priorities, the *tone* of the forward view.
- [ ] Explicit vs. implicit · near-term vs. multi-year framework · standing framework vs. one-off.
- [ ] **Sources:** earnings calls, investor decks, MD&A/annual report, analyst day — and for IPOs the **RHP (a selling document — weight accordingly).**

## B2. Credibility — the say-do ratio
The single most valuable use of guidance: does it come true?
- [ ] Build the **historical beat/miss record** — guided vs. actual, several years. Include stated promises, not just numbers (project timelines, strategic commitments, 3–5 years back).
- [ ] Pattern: chronic **sandbagging** (beats cheaply) · **accurate** · **over-promising** (serial misses)?
- [ ] Did they quietly **change or withdraw** targets when they turned inconvenient?

*Build the say-do table here, once — Step 4 reads it for character and doesn't rebuild it. Here the only question is "should I believe *this* forward claim?"*

## B3. Decode the commentary — read between the lines
How they talk is as informative as what they guide. (This skill has no cleaner home than here.) *Scope here: does the language weaken or qualify this forward claim? What it says about the people → **Step 4**.*
- [ ] **Specificity vs. hedging**; confidence vs. defensiveness.
- [ ] **The dog that didn't bark** — a metric they *stopped* mentioning once it turned down.
- [ ] **Shifting goalposts** — changing which KPIs they emphasize; new bespoke metrics replacing old ones.
- [ ] **Non-answers** to direct analyst questions; euphemisms ("demand normalization," "challenging quarter").
- [ ] **Tone *change*** vs. prior periods — usually more signal than the absolute tone.

## B4. Consistency test — does the claim reconcile with reality? (the heart — where Part B meets Part A)
- [ ] **Vs. the moat (Part A):** does guidance implicitly promise durable returns the moat actually supports? *(If not — trust the moat, flag the gap.)*
- [ ] **Vs. the industry ceiling:** does the implied growth/margin/share exceed what *anyone* in this industry has achieved?
- [ ] **Vs. unit economics & capacity:** is the target physically consistent with unit economics, capacity, capex, order book?
- [ ] **Internally consistent:** revenue vs. capex vs. capacity vs. headcount — do the pieces add up?

## B5. The metrics they chose
What they measure by is a choice, and the choice is a tell.
- [ ] GAAP, or **bespoke/adjusted metrics** that flatter?
- [ ] **Changing definitions** period to period?
- [ ] Are the headline KPIs the ones that actually drive value (Step 5 key drivers), or vanity metrics?

*Here: catalogue which metrics they chose and how definitions changed. Character read → **Step 4**; the math (sizing adjusted-vs-reported gaps) → **Step 5**.*

- The **full management-character verdict** → **Step 4**

## Traps (both parts — re-read before finishing)
**Part A:**
- [ ] Storytelling a moat that isn't in the numbers · halo effect (success → *assume* a moat) · backward-looking (moat *was* real) · overrating brand (awareness ≠ pricing power).

**Part B:**
- [ ] Treating guidance as an input to trust rather than a claim to test.
- [ ] Anchoring your own forecast to management's number.
- [ ] Taking qualitative commentary at face value — it's curated.
- [ ] Rewarding confident delivery (charisma) over the say-do record.

## The verdict (do this last) — two cases + the reconciliation
> **Structural (moat):** [none / emerging / narrow / wide], sourced from ________, currently [widening / stable / eroding], durable ~________.
>
> **Asserted (guidance):** management's forward claim is [credible / mixed / not credible]; say-do record ________.
>
> **Reconciliation:** the moat [does / doesn't] underwrite what guidance promises. Where they diverge: ________________________.

**The punchline:** if guidance promises durability the moat can't support, **trust the moat.** If the moat supports more than guidance admits, management may be **sandbagging** (a possible opportunity). Agreement between the two is the strongest signal of all.

---

## Handoff
- **→ Step 4:** advantages that are really execution; the say-do table (B2), commentary findings (B3), and metric choices (B5) — for Step 4 to read for character, not rebuild.
- **→ Step 5:** confirm ROIC vs. WACC; quantify moat depth; bottom-up forecast vs. guidance; forensic check on chosen metrics.
- **→ Step 6:** the moat verdict is your license to extrapolate (feed direction into terminal assumptions); guided vs. priced-in.
- **→ Step 7:** moat-erosion vectors (A4–A5) and guidance-dependence/credibility as thesis risks, sized there.
- **→ Step 8:** no moat → thesis rests on price/cyclical/catalyst; new capacity/expansion as a dated catalyst.

*Step 2 judged the water. Step 3 judges whether this fish keeps out-swimming the school — structurally, and by its own account — and whether those two stories agree.*
