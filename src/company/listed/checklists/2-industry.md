# Industry Structure — Analyst's Checklist

*Companion to the Business Model checklist (v2 format). Step 2 of the framework.*

> **The one rule for this step:** judge the *pond*, not the *fish*. Step 1 forbade all verdicts; Step 2 **allows a verdict — but only about the industry, never about your company.** "This is a structurally attractive industry" is Step 2's job. "This company is better than its rivals" is the moat (Step 3) and must not leak in. **The test for every sentence you write here: is it true of the *average player* in the industry?** If it's about *your* company specifically, you've crossed into Step 3 — flag it and move on. Keep asking: *am I describing the pond, or the fish?*

### The flag convention (same as v2)
When you notice something that matters but isn't Step 2's to judge, write it as a **fact plus the step that owns it**, then move on.
- **Format:** `Fact → Step N`
- ✅ "Industry-wide switching costs are low; incumbents win on relationships → Step 3 (does *our* company have those relationships?)"
- ❌ "Our company's deep OEM relationships lock customers in" (that's the fish — Step 3)

**Who owns which judgment:** company-specific advantage / moat → **Step 3** · management → **Step 4** · financial quality → **Step 5** · the *severity/probability* of a specific risk → **Step 7** · the differentiated thesis → **Step 8**. Step 2 keeps only the verdict on the *industry's* attractiveness.

**How to use this:** Run every section. The output is a defensible one-line verdict on the pond *plus* a stack of `→ Step 3` flags naming what a moat would have to be made of. Where you can't answer, that gap is a finding — record it.

---

## 1. Define the industry — *what game is actually being played?*
- [ ] Define the industry by the *job* and the set of players competing for it — **not** by the company's own product label.
- [ ] Distinguish the market the company *serves* from the market that *constrains* it (sets its prices, pulls its customers away).
- [ ] **The test:** if this company vanished tomorrow, where would its customers go? That list *is* the industry.

**Boundary:** define the arena *neutrally* first — resist drawing it around the company's strengths (that rigs the pond to flatter the fish). The company's own revenue model was Step 1; here you're mapping the whole field it plays on.

---

## 2. Lifecycle, growth & cyclicality — *the tide the whole industry rides*
- [ ] Where on the S-curve: emerging / growth / mature / declining? How fast is the *industry* growing?
- [ ] Is demand **secular** (a durable structural trend) or **cyclical** (a swing that will mean-revert)? Name which, and roughly where in the cycle we are now.
- [ ] What drives industry-wide demand — GDP, rates, a commodity cycle, a government scheme?

**The trap to internalize:** high growth ≠ high profitability. Fast growth is a *magnet for capital and competition* that competes returns away — treat it as a reason for suspicion until the forces below check out.

**Boundary:** this is the industry's demand curve, not the company's revenue (Step 1) or financials (Step 5).

---

## 3. The five forces — *why are returns in this industry high or low?*
Run each at the **industry level**. The goal isn't five filled boxes — it's finding the **one binding force** that caps profitability.
- [ ] **Rivalry:** how many players, competing on price or differentiation? (High fixed + high exit barriers + commodity product = chronic price war.)
- [ ] **New entrants:** what stops a newcomer — capital, regulation, scale, industry-wide switching costs? *(Barriers to entering the industry — not this company's specific moat.)*
- [ ] **Substitutes:** what *different* product does the same job? (Links to Step 1's "real competition.")
- [ ] **Buyer power:** customers concentrated / price-sensitive / able to squeeze? (Government L1 lowest-bidder tendering is buyer power in its most brutal form.)
- [ ] **Supplier power:** key inputs concentrated or critical enough that suppliers capture the margin?
- [ ] **Name the binding force:** which single one is doing the most to cap returns here?

**Boundary:** every force describes the *average* participant. "Buyers can squeeze this industry" is Step 2; "but *our* company resists it via switching costs" → **Step 3**.

---

## 4. Structure & competitive rationality — *fragmented or consolidated, and do they compete like adults?*
- [ ] Concentration: fragmented (many small, usually price-competitive, low-return) vs. consolidated oligopoly (few, often more rational). And the *trajectory* — consolidating or fragmenting?
- [ ] Rationality: do players hold price discipline, or race to the bottom?
- [ ] **The test:** when demand softens, does this industry cut price to fill capacity, or hold the line? (Predicts through-cycle profitability better than almost anything.)

**Boundary:** this is the *industry's* concentration — distinct from the *company's own* customer/supplier concentration (that was Step 1 → Step 7).

---

## 5. Profit pools & value chain — *where along the chain does the money actually pool?*
- [ ] Map the full vertical chain: raw input → each intermediate step → end customer.
- [ ] Ask where profit *accumulates* — often **not** where the revenue or the visible activity is. Compare the *revenue* pool to the *profit* pool along the chain.
- [ ] Locate which slice your company sits in.

**Boundary:** whether the *industry's slice* is profitable is Step 2; whether *your company captures more than its peers within that slice* → **Step 3**. (Being in a giant industry means nothing if you're in the commodity-margin link.)

---

## 6. Basis of competition / key success factors — *what does it take to win here?*
- [ ] Name what structurally determines winners and losers in this industry: scale, technology, distribution, relationships/empanelment, lowest cost, brand.
- [ ] **The test:** to build a new winner from scratch, what would be the hardest thing to replicate? That's the raw material of every moat in this industry.

**Boundary — this is the cleanest hand-off to Step 3:** naming the success factor is Step 2; judging whether *this* company possesses it is Step 3. Name it as a `→ Step 3` flag; don't grade the company on it yet.

---

## 7. Regulation & policy exposure — *is the game shaped by the state?*
- [ ] Is the industry *created/inflated* by policy (a subsidy scheme), *governed* by it (public tendering, customs, licensing), or *threatened* by it? (Tailwind and dependency are the same fact from two angles.)
- [ ] Durability: is the policy a multi-year structural commitment, or a scheme that can lapse or reverse?

**Boundary:** the *structural role* of regulation is Step 2; the *severity and probability* of a specific regulatory risk to your thesis → **Step 7**. Note it here, size it there.

---

## 8. Structural trajectory & disruption — *is the structure stable or moving?*
- [ ] Scan disruption vectors: technology (platforms disintermediating middlemen), business-model shifts, deregulation, new entrants changing the basis of competition.
- [ ] **The test:** are the barriers and profit pools you just mapped getting *stronger or weaker* over the next ~5 years?

**Boundary:** the structural trajectory is Step 2; whether a *specific* disruption breaks *your* thesis, and how likely, → **Step 7**. (An industry with great current economics that's structurally eroding is a value trap in slow motion.)

---

## Traps specific to this step (re-read before you finish)
- [ ] Defining the industry to flatter the company.
- [ ] Filling five boxes instead of finding the one binding force.
- [ ] Confusing industry *growth* with industry *attractiveness* (the big one).
- [ ] Reading structure as a static snapshot when the *trajectory* is what pays you.
- [ ] Letting "this is a good company" sneak in where "is this a good industry" belongs. *If you can't say it about the average player, it doesn't go here.*

---

## The industry verdict (do this last)
State it in one line — the analog of Step 1's two-sentence test:

> This industry is **[structurally attractive / mediocre / value-destroying]**, and the binding reason is __________________________.

If you can't name the *binding reason*, you've mapped the pond but not judged it. Go back to Section 3.

---

## Handoff to the rest of the framework
Step 2 produces **one verdict on the pond + a stack of parked flags.** Everything you tagged `→ Step N` collects here, grouped by owner step:

- **→ Step 3 (moat):** the industry's key success factors (what a moat must be made of); any place you noticed the company resisting a force better than peers; whether it captures more than its slice.
- **→ Step 5 (financials):** whether the industry's economics show up in the company's numbers; cyclical vs. structural demand to test against reported growth.
- **→ Step 7 (risks):** regulatory dependency severity; specific disruption probability; buyer/supplier squeeze risk.
- **→ Step 8 (thesis):** if the pond is only average, the thesis will have to rest entirely on the fish — note that now.

*Judge the water here → judge the fish next.*
