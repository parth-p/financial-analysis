# Differentiated Thesis, Catalyst & Time Horizon — Analyst's Checklist

*Companion to Steps 1–7. Step 8 of the framework — the capstone.*

> **The one rule for this step:** this is the **wrapper, not a summary.** A summary says "here's what I learned about this company." A thesis says **"here's where I disagree with the price, here's why I'm right, and here's what makes the market come around."** If your output recaps Steps 1–7, you have a book report, not an investment.
>
> **The first test is brutal: does your conclusion differ from what's priced in (Step 6)?** A correct-but-consensus view makes no money — the market already charges for it. The prize requires being **variant *and* right** — and both halves are hard.
>
> **If you can't name your edge, you don't have one — buy the index.** That sentence is the whole discipline.
>
> **This is the terminal step.** Its handoff is not to another step — it's to a **decision** (buy / pass / size) and to the **monitoring loop.**

### The flag convention (now closing the loop)
Every prior step's flags land here. Pull them together:
- Step 6's gap = the raw material of the thesis · Step 7's kill criteria = the thesis-broken exit · Step 3's moat = whether it can let time be its catalyst · Step 5's key drivers = what a catalyst inflects.

**How to use this:** thesis not summary; name the edge or pass; variant *and* correct; pre-commit the exits while unanchored; "pass" is a valid and common answer. Output is a one-sentence thesis + edge + catalyst + horizon + exits + size — *or* a clean "pass."

---

## 1. State the differentiated thesis (the core)
- [ ] In 1–2 sentences: what you believe, how it differs from what's priced, why you're right.
- [ ] Use the template: *"The market believes X (priced in). I believe Y. Steps 1–5 say I'm right. Step 6 says that's worth [gap]."*
- [ ] **The gate:** does it differ from consensus/price? If it matches, you found *no opportunity* — go to Section 7.

---

## 2. Name the edge — *why is this available to me?*
- [ ] Which kind? **Informational** (know something others don't — rare) · **Analytical** (interpret public facts better) · **Behavioral/temperamental** (hold through discomfort, act when others are forced to sell — the most durable and available).
- [ ] **Why does the mispricing persist?** Too small / boring / controversial / forced selling / time-horizon mismatch / genuine complexity?
- [ ] **If you can't name the edge → pass / buy the index.**

---

## 3. The catalyst — *what closes the gap, and roughly when*
- [ ] Type: **operational** (product cycle, margin inflection) · **corporate action** (spinoff, buyback, M&A — cleanest, dated & self-inflicted) · **external** (regulation, litigation, cycle turn) · **re-rating trigger** (activist, index inclusion, disclosure change).
- [ ] **Hard** (dated, near-certain to happen — horizon knowable) or **soft** (hoped-for inflection, no clock)?
- [ ] **Two cautions:** the *obvious* catalyst is usually already priced; and *not all growth is a catalyst worth wanting* — always ask "at what return on capital?"

**The rule:** catalysts matter in proportion to how much you care about *when*. Cheap + **great** can let time be its catalyst; cheap + **mediocre** needs an external catalyst or it's a value trap.

---

## 4. Time horizon — *over what period, and can you hold it?*
- [ ] State the horizon explicitly (it determines how much catalysts matter and what "wrong" looks like).
- [ ] Match it to your capital: patient capital can wait; time-bound capital (redemptions, leverage, impatience) needs catalysts.
- [ ] **IRR check:** being right in year 5 is a mediocre annualized return. Dead money is a cost.

---

## 5. Sell discipline — *both exits, pre-committed*
Define both *before* buying, while unanchored:
- [ ] **Thesis-achieved exit:** price reaches value / re-rates / a materially better opportunity appears / the reason you bought is no longer true (because it worked).
- [ ] **Thesis-broken exit:** the kill criteria from **Step 7.**
- [ ] **The crucial separation:** "the price fell" (not a sell reason if thesis intact — maybe buy) vs. "the thesis broke" (sell regardless of price).

---

## 6. Position sizing — *conviction, not enthusiasm*
- [ ] Size = quality of edge × margin of safety (Step 6) ÷ risk & permanence (Step 7).
- [ ] Size **up** with a nameable edge and wide margin; **down** with uncertainty and fat left tails.
- [ ] Conviction from a named edge + margin is durable; conviction from a good story evaporates in the first drawdown.

---

## 7. The pass test — *is there actually an opportunity?* (the honest default)
- [ ] Could the honest answer be **"pass"** — great business, fair price, no variant perception?
- [ ] **Most careful analysis should end here.** Walking away when there's no edge *is* the process working, not a failure of it.
- [ ] "No opportunity" is legitimate, common, and often the *most* correct conclusion.

---

## Write it down — *before you buy*
- [ ] Commit thesis, edge, catalyst, horizon, exits, and size to paper *in advance.*
- [ ] This is the record you'll audit against — the only real defense against hindsight rewriting your reasons.

---

## Traps specific to this step (re-read before finishing)
- [ ] **The master trap:** confusing a summary with a thesis.
- [ ] Investing with no nameable edge (the most common, most expensive error — activity for its own sake).
- [ ] A consensus thesis (right but priced in — no money).
- [ ] Catalyst-chasing on a mediocre business; buying a soft/undated catalyst that's already priced.
- [ ] Falling in love with the thesis (pre-mortem & kill criteria guard against it).
- [ ] Ignoring horizon / IRR — being right too slowly.
- [ ] No sell discipline — round-tripping winners, riding broken theses down.
- [ ] Oversizing a thin edge.
- [ ] Mistaking a great *story* for a variant perception — if everyone loves it, it's priced in.

---

## The thesis verdict (do this last)
State it in one breath — the culmination of all eight verdict tests:

> **I believe** ______________, **which the market doesn't because** ______________.
> **My edge is** [informational / analytical / behavioral], **and the mispricing persists because** ______________.
> **The catalyst is** ______________ [hard / soft], **over a horizon of** ______________.
> **I'll exit when** [achieved: ______] **or** [broken: ______].
> **Position size:** ______________.
>
> **— OR —**
>
> **Pass:** ________________________ (no edge / fairly priced / outside my circle of competence).

If you can't answer *"what do I believe that the market doesn't, why am I right, what makes them come around, and by when"* — the honest verdict is **pass, buy the index.**

---

## The loop (this is the end, and the beginning)
Step 8 hands off to **action and monitoring**, not another analysis step:

- **To action:** buy at a size set by edge × margin ÷ risk — or pass.
- **To the monitoring loop:** the thesis, catalyst, and kill criteria become a live watchlist. New facts re-open the relevant step — a moat crack re-opens Step 3, a governance event re-opens Step 4, a price move re-opens Step 6. **The framework isn't a line, it's a loop you re-run as reality changes.**

---

### How the eight steps come together
- **Steps 1–5** build your view of *reality* (business → industry → moat → management → financials).
- **Step 6** collides that view with *price* and measures the gap.
- **Step 7** stress-tests it — how it dies, and whether the price pays you.
- **Step 8** states your *edge* and puts a *clock* on it — or concludes there's no edge.

*The amateur fills eight bins and calls it done. The pro is hunting the single place where their view of reality and the market's diverge — everything else was just the work required to earn a defensible opinion on that one gap.*
