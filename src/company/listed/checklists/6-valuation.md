# Valuation vs. What's Priced In — Analyst's Checklist

*Companion to the Business Model (v2), Industry Structure, Moat, Management, and Financials checklists. Step 6 of the framework.*

> **The one rule for this step:** valuation is the **collision** of your view of reality (Steps 1–5) with the market's price — and it is **meaningless in isolation.** A 40x multiple can be cheap and an 8x expensive; which one depends entirely on the growth, returns, and durability you already established. You *cannot* do this step before Steps 1–5.
>
> **The great reframe — ask what's priced in, not what it's worth.** "What is it worth?" invites you to reverse-engineer a number you already wanted. Instead, take the price as given and back out **what it assumes must be true**, then judge those embedded expectations against Steps 1–5. Price is the market's consensus forecast made numerical; your job is to find where *your* forecast differs — and in your favor.
>
> **Boundary:** Step 6 establishes the *gap*. It is **not** risk enumeration (**Step 7** — though your margin of safety responds to risk) and **not** timing (whether/when the gap closes is **Step 8**'s catalyst question).

### The flag convention (same as before)
- **Format:** `Fact → Step N`
- ✅ "Price implies 22% growth for 10 yrs — probability and what breaks it → Step 7"
- ✅ "Gap is +60% to base case but needs a re-rating trigger → Step 8"

**Who owns which judgment:** what could break the thesis & how likely → **Step 7** · what makes the market come around and by when → **Step 8**.

**Incoming flags to use here:** Step 2's industry ceilings (feasible margins/share) · Step 3's moat *direction* (sets the competitive-advantage period / fade) · Step 4's allocation quality (a dollar reinvested badly isn't worth a dollar) · Step 5's key drivers, ROIC-vs-WACC spread, and normalized earnings power.

**How to use this:** invert (what's priced in), range not point, always relative to your view. Output is the gap stated plainly + a margin-of-safety verdict.

---

## 1. Precondition — *do you actually have a view?*
- [ ] Can you state, from Steps 1–5, roughly what this business will earn and *why*?
- [ ] If not — **stop.** Valuation is the last analytical step for a reason; you can't value what you don't understand.

---

## 2. Normalize the earnings power — *fix the denominator first*
Every method is only as honest as the earnings figure underneath it.
- [ ] Normalize to **through-cycle, sustainable** earnings power — strip genuine one-offs, adjust for cyclical position.
- [ ] Prefer **owner earnings** (≈ net income + D&A − maintenance capex, ± working capital) over reported net income.
- [ ] **The cyclical trap:** a cyclical at *trough* earnings looks expensive (and is often cheapest); at *peak* earnings looks cheap (and is often most dangerous).

*(Feeds every method below. Uses Step 5's earnings-quality work.)*

---

## 3. What's priced in — *the reverse DCF* (the anchor)
- [ ] Back out what today's price implies: what **growth**, what **margin**, what **return on capital**, and for **how long** excess returns persist?
- [ ] Judge those embedded expectations against Steps 1–5 — conservative, reasonable, or heroic?

**The question:** what has to go right for this price to make sense, and how likely is that? *(If the price implies a growth/margin no one in the industry — Step 2 — has ever achieved, you've found something.)*

---

## 4. Intrinsic value — *DCF as a range, never a point*
A thinking tool, not a precision instrument.
- [ ] **Scenarios, not a point:** bear / base / bull, roughly probability-weighted. (A single-point DCF is a lie told to two decimals.)
- [ ] **Terminal value = where the moat feeds in.** It usually dominates; it hinges on the **competitive-advantage period** (how long ROIC > WACC). Step 3's moat *direction* sets the fade — wide/widening earns a long CAP, eroding earns a short one.
- [ ] **Sensitivity on the Step 5 key drivers** — the value is the point; find the 2–3 assumptions it actually hinges on.

---

## 5. Relative valuation — *multiples, always in context*
Shorthand for a DCF; useful for triangulation.
- [ ] Pick the right multiple for the business: **EV/EBIT** across capital structures, **P/B** for financials, **EV/Sales** for not-yet-profitable, P/E / P/FCF / EV/EBITDA as fits. Use **enterprise value** to compare across balance sheets.
- [ ] Context is everything: vs. peers, vs. own history, and vs. what **fundamentals justify** (high-ROIC/wide-moat *deserves* a premium; a low multiple can be a value trap).
- [ ] Remember: relative valuation only says cheap/expensive *vs. the peer group* — the whole group can be mispriced.

---

## 6. Triangulate & sanity-check
- [ ] Do the methods converge on a rough range? When DCF and multiples disagree sharply, find out *why* (different embedded assumptions — informative in itself).
- [ ] **Reality tests linked to earlier steps:** what market share does the price imply — is it possible? What margin does it require — has *anyone* in this industry (Step 2) achieved it?
- [ ] **Reverse stress test:** at what price would this be *obviously* cheap / *obviously* expensive?
- [ ] Sum-of-the-parts if multi-segment.

---

## 7. Margin of safety — *the gap, scaled to certainty*
- [ ] Is the discount of price to your value *range* wide enough to absorb errors and bad luck?
- [ ] **Scale the cushion inversely to quality/predictability:** a wide-moat compounder needs less; a fragile cyclical needs more.
- [ ] Resolve the founding principle: *a great company at an absurd price is a bad investment.* Quality changes how much you can pay — it doesn't exempt you from price discipline.

---

## Traps specific to this step (re-read before finishing)
- [ ] False precision — a two-decimal DCF on guessed inputs. (Better roughly right than precisely wrong.)
- [ ] Reverse-engineering the target you already wanted (the reverse DCF is the antidote).
- [ ] Anchoring on the current price, the 52-week range, or your own purchase price.
- [ ] Multiples without context; the whole peer group mispriced together.
- [ ] The cyclical trap (trough looks dear, peak looks cheap).
- [ ] Terminal value quietly doing 80% of the work on a hand-wave.
- [ ] Confusing a good *company* with a good *investment*.

---

## The valuation verdict (do this last)
State the gap plainly — the analog of the earlier verdict tests:

> **What the price implies:** ______________ (growth / margin / duration, from the reverse DCF).
> **My view vs. that:** the market is too [optimistic / pessimistic] because ________________________.
> **Value range:** bear ____ / base ____ / bull ____.
> **Margin of safety at today's price:** [adequate / thin / none], and scaled to this business's quality it [is / isn't] enough.

If your "value" is a single number, or you can't name what the market believes that you think is wrong, you did arithmetic, not valuation. Go back to Section 3.

---

## Handoff to the rest of the framework
Step 6 produces the gap + a margin-of-safety verdict. Everything tagged `→ Step N` collects here:

- **→ Step 7 (risks):** the embedded expectations most likely to break; how much of the margin of safety a bad scenario consumes; which key driver, if wrong, closes the gap the wrong way.
- **→ Step 8 (thesis & catalyst):** the gap *is* the variant perception in arithmetic form — but a gap with no catalyst can stay a gap for years. Hand over: what the market is missing, and the open question of what makes it come around, and by when.

*Steps 1–5 built the view of reality. Step 6 collided it with price and measured the gap. Step 7 stress-tests it; Step 8 puts a clock on it.*
